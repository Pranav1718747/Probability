Academic Abstract

Numerical data integrity is essential across forensic accounting, tax compliance, scientific publishing, and macroeconomic surveillance. When unauthorized actors fabricate records, human cognitive biases—specifically the equal-likelihood fallacy and avoidance of digit repetition—frequently cause invented numbers to diverge from naturally occurring logarithmic digit distributions. This investigation develops and executes an automated statistical screening framework grounded in Module 6 (Hypothesis Testing-II) of the BMAT202 syllabus, operationalizing Pearson's Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit test (χ²) to evaluate conformity against Benford's Law (P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d) for d ∈ {1, …, 9}).

We evaluated three empirical datasets under rigorous testing protocols: (1) an authentic empirical dataset comprising n = 217 sovereign nation populations obtained from the World Bank's 2023 World Development Indicators (spanning five orders of magnitude); (2) a simulated fabricated dataset of equal sample size (n = 217) reflecting human cognitive uniform-digit heuristics; and (3) a historical reference dataset of 141 North American river lengths compiled in standard R. Under a significance level of α = 0.05 with df = 8 (χ²crit = 15.5073), the genuine demographic dataset demonstrated close conformity to Benford's Law (χ² = 7.6083, p = 0.4726, Mean Absolute Deviation MAD = 0.01521), resulting in a definitive failure to reject the null hypothesis H0. Conversely, the simulated fabricated dataset exhibited severe statistical departure (χ² = 79.9819, p = 4.93 × 10-14, MAD = 0.06211), rejecting H0 with overwhelming significance. Notably, the historical river lengths dataset also rejected H0 (χ² = 44.1766, p = 5.27 × 10-7), exposing the critical statistical reality that lower-bound selection truncation destroys natural scale invariance. We synthesize these empirical discoveries into a 5-stage responsible forensic audit workflow that leverages chi-square residual diagnostics for targeted investigative sampling without generating false accusations.

Keywords: Benford's Law, Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test, Hypothesis Testing, Forensic Accounting, Data Fabrication Detection, Cochran's Rule, Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), World Bank Data, R Programming.

1. Introduction and Background

1.1 The Imperative of Numerical Data Integrity

In an increasingly data-driven global economy, decisions involving billions of dollars in capital allocation, governmental fiscal policy, corporate compliance, and peer-reviewed scientific discovery hinge upon the authenticity of underlying numerical records. However, modern auditing environments face a staggering volume of data: corporate general ledgers contain millions of journal entries, revenue authorities process hundreds of millions of tax filings, and research databases index thousands of empirical studies weekly. In such high-dimensional environments, line-by-line substantive manual auditing is mathematically and economically infeasible.

Consequently, auditors, regulatory watchdogs, and forensic investigators require automated, computationally lightweight, non-invasive statistical screening procedures that can rapidly triage entire numerical populations, highlighting high-risk ledgers for targeted human inspection while certifying low-risk distributions as statistically unexceptional.

1.2 The Phenomenon of Benford's Law

First observed by astronomer Simon Newcomb in 1881—who noticed that earlier pages of logarithm tables were systematically more soiled and worn than later pages—and systematically formalized empirically by physicist Frank Benford in 1938, the First-Digit Law (or Law of Anomalous Numbers) dictates that in naturally occurring numerical datasets spanning multiple orders of magnitude, the leading significant digit d ∈ {1, 2, …, 9} does not occur with uniform probability (1/9 ≈ 11.1%). Instead, it follows a strict logarithmic decay function:

P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d) = log10(d + 1) - log10(d),      d ∈ {1, 2, …, 9}

Under this continuous logarithmic measure, numbers beginning with digit 1 appear approximately 30.1% of the time, while numbers beginning with digit 9 occur only 4.6% of the time. Mathematical foundations established by Pinkham (1961) and Hill (1995) proved that Benford's Law is the unique probability distribution on significant digits that exhibits both scale invariance (the distribution remains invariant under multiplication by any non-zero constant, such as currency conversion) and base invariance. Hill further proved that when numbers are sampled randomly from a mixture of diverse, independent probability distributions, the composite distribution asymptotically converges to Benford's Law.

1.3 Real-Life Problem Statement and Practical Significance

When human agents invent numbers—whether deliberately committing procurement embezzlement, fabricating scientific laboratory trials, padding corporate travel expenses, or evading customs tariffs—they rarely consult mathematical distribution theory. Instead, humans are governed by a well-documented cognitive heuristic termed the equal-likelihood bias (or uniform digit fallacy). Humans intuitively believe that "random" numbers should look evenly dispersed across digits 1 through 9. Furthermore, fabricators suffer from psychological aversion to repeating digit 1, viewing repeated leading ones as "suspicious" or "cluttered."

This fundamental cognitive discrepancy establishes a powerful diagnostic tension:

  • Natural Systems: Multiplicative growth, geometric compounding, and unconstrained physical metrics naturally satisfy logarithmic spacing (∼ 30.1% ones).
  • Invented Systems: Human fabrication skews toward equal digit frequencies (∼ 11.1% across all digits) or over-represents psychologically salient digits (such as 5, 7, and 8).

The practical problem addressed in this study is: Can an undergraduate-level hypothesis testing framework—specifically Pearson's Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit test implemented in R—reliably distinguish authentic real-world data from cognitively fabricated numbers, while maintaining strict awareness of sample size constraints, selection boundaries, and inferential limitations?

2.2 Formulation of Statistical Hypotheses

Let X denote a discrete random variable representing the first significant decimal digit of an observation, where the support of X is S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9}. The hypothesis testing problem is formally structured as:

H0: P(X = d) = pd = log10(1 + 1/d),      ∀ d ∈ {1, 2, …, 9}
H1: P(X = d) ≠ log10(1 + 1/d)      for at least one d ∈ {1, 2, …, 9}

The null hypothesis H0 asserts complete conformity to Benford's Law. The alternative hypothesis H1 is non-directional (omnibus), stating that the underlying generative process produces leading digits that depart from the theoretical logarithmic distribution.

2.3 The Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit Test Statistic

To test H0 against H1, we partition a sample of n independent observations into k = 9 mutually exclusive and exhaustive digit bins. Let Oi denote the observed frequency of digit i, and let Ei denote the expected frequency under H0:

Ei = n · pi = n · log10(1 + 1/i),      i ∈ {1, 2, …, 9}

The discrepancy between observed counts and theoretical expectations is measured using Pearson's chi-square test statistic:

χ² = ∑i=19 (Oi - Ei)² / Ei

Under H0, as n &to; ∞, the distribution of χ² converges asymptotically to a continuous chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom df:

df = k - 1 - m

where k = 9 is the number of categorical bins, and m is the number of distribution parameters estimated from the sample data. Because Benford's Law is a completely specified mathematical distribution with fixed theoretical constants (p1 ≈ 0.301, …, p9 ≈ 0.046), no parameters are estimated from the sample (m = 0). The loss of one degree of freedom arises solely from the linear constraint that observed counts must sum to the total sample size (∑ Oi = n). Thus:

df = 9 - 1 - 0 = 8

2.4 Significance Level, Critical Region, and Decision Rule

We establish an explicit significance level of α = 0.05. The critical value χ²α, df represents the (1 - α)-th quantile of the central χ² distribution with 8 degrees of freedom:

χ²0.05, 8 ≈ 15.5073

The decision rule is formulated as follows:

  • If χ² > 15.5073 (or equivalently, if p-value < 0.05), Reject H0 at α = 0.05. Conclude that the observed first-digit distribution departs significantly from Benford's Law.
  • If χ² ≤ 15.5073 (or equivalently, if p-value ≥ 0.05), Fail to Reject H0 at α = 0.05. Conclude that there is insufficient statistical evidence to reject conformity to Benford's Law.

2.5 Cochran's Expected-Count Condition

The asymptotic convergence of Pearson's multinomial test statistic to the continuous χ² distribution relies on Cochran's classical sampling criteria (Cochran, 1952, 1954):

Cochran's Rule: The asymptotic chi-square approximation is valid provided that no expected cell frequency is less than 1 (Ei ≥ 1 for all i), and no more than 20% of the cells have expected frequencies less than 5 (Ei ≥ 5 for at least 80% of categories).

In a 9-cell Benford test, the least frequent digit is 9, with theoretical probability p9 = log10(10/9) ≈ 0.045757. To satisfy the condition that all cells satisfy Ei ≥ 5, the minimum sample size is:

n ≥ 5 / p9 ≈ 5 / 0.045757 ≈ 109.27 &implies; n ≥ 110

In our study, the primary World Bank dataset contains n = 217 observations, yielding a minimum expected count of E9 = 217 × 0.045757 = 9.93 > 5.0. Therefore, 100% of the bins satisfy Cochran's rule with generous margin, completely eliminating any requirement to collapse digit categories.

2.6 Type I and Type II Error Dynamics

  • Type I Error (α = 0.05): False positive. An authentic dataset is flagged as anomalous. In a commercial audit, this triggers costly, disruptive forensic inquiries into compliant departments, squandering audit resources. We fix α = 0.05 to cap this error probability at 5%.
  • Type II Error (β): False negative. A fabricated or fraudulent dataset is erroneously certified as compliant. This allows financial manipulation, tax fraud, or scientific falsification to escape detection. The power of the test (1 - β) increases with sample size n and effect size.

2.7 Effect Size: Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)

A critical limitation of Pearson's chi-square test in large samples is its power sensitivity: because χ² is proportional to n, very large datasets produce massive χ² values even for trivial deviations. To decouple practical significance from sample size, Nigrini (2012) established the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) of digit proportions:

MAD = (1/9) ∑i=19 |Oi/n - pi| = (1/9) ∑i=19 |pobs, i - pexp, i|

# SECTION B: ROBUST FIRST SIGNIFICANT DIGIT EXTRACTION FUNCTION
# Handles: positive floats, large numbers, decimals, scientific notation,
#          zeros (excluded), negative numbers (sign stripped/absolute value),
#          and missing/NA/NaN values.
# ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
extract_first_digit <- function(x, dataset_name = "Dataset") {
  initial_count <- length(x)
  
  # Step 1: Remove NA, NaN, NULL, non-finite
  valid_mask <- !is.na(x) & !is.nan(x) & is.finite(x)
  x_clean <- x[valid_mask]
  
  # Step 2: Handle non-positive numbers (Benford's law requires positive magnitude)
  # Take absolute values of non-zero entries
  x_pos <- abs(x_clean[x_clean != 0])
  
  removed_count <- initial_count - length(x_pos)
  if (removed_count > 0) {
    cat(sprintf("[CLEAN] '%s': Excluded %d invalid/zero/missing observations out of %d (%.2f%%).\n",
                dataset_name, removed_count, initial_count, (removed_count / initial_count) * 100))
  }
  
  if (length(x_pos) == 0) {
    warning(sprintf("'%s' has 0 valid positive observations after cleaning!", dataset_name))
    return(integer(0))
  }
  
  # Step 3: Extract leading non-zero digit reliably using scientific representation
  # Format in exponential form (e.g., 0.0042 -> 4.200000e-03; 1438069596 -> 1.438070e+09)
  # The first character of this string is guaranteed to be the first significant digit (1-9).
  exp_strings <- formatC(x_pos, format = "e", digits = 10)
  first_digits <- as.integer(substr(exp_strings, 1, 1))
  
  # Sanity check: all digits must be in 1..9
  stopifnot(all(first_digits %in% 1:9))
  return(first_digits)
}

}

# SECTION C, D & E: COMPREHENSIVE CHI-SQUARE GOODNESS-OF-FIT ANALYSIS ENGINE
# ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
analyze_benford <- function(data_vector, label = "Dataset", alpha = 0.05) {
  d <- extract_first_digit(data_vector, label)
  n <- length(d)
  
  if (n == 0) {
    stop(sprintf("Cannot perform goodness-of-fit test on '%s': sample size is 0.", label))
  }
  
  # Tabulate observed frequencies across digits 1 through 9
  obs <- tabulate(d, nbins = 9)
  names(obs) <- 1:9
  
  # Theoretical expected counts
  exp_counts <- n * benford_p
  
  # Check Cochran's rule (E_i >= 5)
  min_exp <- min(exp_counts)
  cochran_violation <- min_exp < 5
  
  # Calculate chi-square statistic: sum((O_i - E_i)^2 / E_i)
  diffs <- obs - exp_counts
  contributions <- (diffs^2) / exp_counts
  chi_sq_stat_manual <- sum(contributions)
  
  # Degrees of freedom: k - 1 - p (where k=9 categories, p=0 estimated parameters)
  df <- 9 - 1
  
  # Critical value from chi-square distribution at alpha = 0.05
  crit_val <- qchisq(1 - alpha, df = df)
  
  # Built-in R chisq.test
  r_test <- chisq.test(obs, p = benford_p, rescale.p = TRUE)
  chi_sq_stat_r <- as.numeric(r_test$statistic)
  p_val_r <- as.numeric(r_test$p.value)
  
  # Manual p-value verification via survival function pchisq(..., lower.tail = FALSE)
  p_val_manual <- pchisq(chi_sq_stat_manual, df = df, lower.tail = FALSE)
  
  # Verify internal numerical consistency between manual and built-in formulas
  tolerance <- 1e-7
  stopifnot(abs(chi_sq_stat_manual - chi_sq_stat_r) < tolerance)
  stopifnot(abs(p_val_manual - p_val_r) < tolerance)
  
  # Effect size: Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) of proportions
  obs_props <- obs / n
  exp_props <- benford_p
  mad <- mean(abs(obs_props - exp_props))
  
  # Comprehensive frequency table
  freq_table <- data.frame(
    Digit                = 1:9,
    Observed_Count       = obs,
    Expected_Count       = round(exp_counts, 4),
    Observed_Proportion  = round(obs_props, 5),
    Expected_Proportion  = round(exp_props, 5),
    O_minus_E            = round(diffs, 4),
    ChiSquare_Contrib    = round(contributions, 5)
  )
  
  # Hypothesis decision at alpha
  decision <- if (chi_sq_stat_r > crit_val) {
    "Reject H0 (Distribution departs significantly from Benford's Law)"
  } else {
    "Fail to Reject H0 (Distribution is consistent with Benford's Law)"
  }
  
  # Console Reporting
  cat("====================================================================\n")
  cat(sprintf(" STATISTICAL AUDIT REPORT: %s\n", toupper(label)))
  cat("====================================================================\n")
  cat(sprintf(" Valid Sample Size (n)       : %d\n", n))
  cat(sprintf(" Chi-Square Statistic (χ²)   : %.4f (Manual: %.4f)\n", chi_sq_stat_r, chi_sq_stat_manual))
  cat(sprintf(" Degrees of Freedom (df)     : %d\n", df))
  cat(sprintf(" Significance Level (α)      : %.2f\n", alpha))
  cat(sprintf(" Critical Value (χ²_crit)    : %.4f\n", crit_val))
  cat(sprintf(" P-Value                     : %.6e\n", p_val_r))
  cat(sprintf(" Mean Absolute Deviation(MAD): %.5f\n", mad))
  cat(sprintf(" Minimum Expected Count      : %.2f (Rule >= 5: %s)\n", 
              min_exp, ifelse(cochran_violation, "WARNING: VIOLATED", "PASSED")))
  cat(sprintf(" Formal Statistical Decision : %s\n", decision))
  cat("--------------------------------------------------------------------\n")
  print(freq_table, row.names = FALSE)
  cat("====================================================================\n\n")
  
  return(list(
    label        = label,
    n            = n,
    table        = freq_table,
    chisq_stat   = chi_sq_stat_r,
    df           = df,
    crit_val     = crit_val,
    p_value      = p_val_r,
    mad          = mad,
    decision     = decision,
    min_exp      = min_exp,
    cochran_ok   = !cochran_violation,
    r_test       = r_test
  ))
}

  # Grouped/Overlay bar plot
  bp <- barplot(obs_p, names.arg = 1:9, col = color_bar,
                border = NA, ylim = c(0, max_y),
                xlab = "First Significant Digit (d)",
                ylab = "Relative Frequency (Proportion)",
                main = sprintf("%s: Observed vs Benford's Law%s", result$label, title_extra),
                font.main = 2, font.lab = 2, cex.main = 1.1, cex.lab = 1.0)
  
  # Add subtle grid lines
  grid(nx = NA, ny = NULL, col = "#E5E7EB", lty = "solid")
  
  # Redraw bars over grid
  barplot(obs_p, names.arg = 1:9, col = color_bar, border = NA, 
          ylim = c(0, max_y), add = TRUE)

  lines(bp, exp_p, type = "b", pch = 18, col = "#DC2626", lwd = 2.5, cex = 1.4)

         legend = c("Observed Proportion", "Benford Theoretical Expectation"),

6. Discussion and Forensic Analysis

6.1 Interpretation of Genuine Data Results (World Bank Demographic Data)

The analysis of the 2023 World Bank sovereign population dataset (n = 217) yielded a test statistic of χ² = 7.6083. With 8 degrees of freedom, the associated p-value is 0.4726 (47.26%). Because χ² = 7.6083 < 15.5073 (and p = 0.4726 > 0.05), we fail to reject the null hypothesis H0.

This finding confirms that the observed leading-digit distribution of national populations across the globe conforms closely to Benford's logarithmic decay. Observed relative frequencies mirror theoretical expectations across the distribution: digit 1 appeared in 31.34% of countries (expected: 30.10%), digit 2 in 16.59% (expected: 17.61%), and digit 9 in 3.69% (expected: 4.58%). The total Mean Absolute Deviation was MAD = 0.01521, which is well within normal random sampling variation for n ≈ 200.

6.2 Interpretation of Fabricated Data Results (Cognitive Bias Model)

In stark contrast, the simulated fabricated dataset (n = 217) yielded χ² = 79.9819, more than five times the critical cutoff of 15.5073. The corresponding p-value is 4.93 × 10-14, leading to an unconditional, overwhelming rejection of H0.

  • Digit 1 Deficit (χ² contribution = 19.10): Only 30 values began with digit 1 (13.82%), compared to the Benford expectation of 65.32 (30.10%). This single cell accounted for 23.9% of the total test statistic.
  • Digit 7 Surplus (χ² contribution = 16.51): 27 values began with digit 7 (12.44%), more than double the expected frequency of 12.58 (5.80%).
  • Digits 5, 8, and 9 Surpluses (χ² contributions = 11.11, 8.83, 10.21): Human fabricators systematically over-allocated numbers to higher digits.
  • Effect Size Surge: The MAD surged to 0.06211—over four times greater than that of the authentic dataset—placing it deep within Nigrini's "Severe Non-Conformity" diagnostic category.

6.3 The River Lengths Paradox: Selection Truncation vs. Scale Invariance

The historical North American river lengths dataset (`rivers`, n = 141) provides a profoundly instructive academic lesson. The chi-square test yielded χ² = 44.1766 (p = 5.27 × 10-7), rejecting H0. Does this mean that the USGS fabricated river lengths in 1975?

Certainly not. The rejection stems from a violation of Benford's fundamental requirement: untruncated scale invariance spanning multiple orders of magnitude. In the *World Almanac* compilation from which the dataset was extracted, only "major" rivers exceeding approximately 135 miles were recorded. Small streams and tributaries (which would naturally supply abundant lengths beginning with digit 1, such as 10–19 miles or 100–199 miles) were systematically omitted. Because of this artificial cutoff, observations cluster heavily between 200 and 400 miles (digit 3 occurred 36 times vs expected 17.62; digit 2 occurred 31 times vs expected 24.83). This lower truncation destroys base-10 scale invariance, causing the sample to fail Benford's test despite consisting of 100% genuine geographical measurements.

7. Proposed Practical Solution: Automated Forensic Audit Screening Workflow

Based on our empirical findings, we propose a standardized, responsible 5-Stage Forensic Screening Framework designed for integration into enterprise audit management systems, tax oversight platforms, and forensic accounting protocols.

Core Ethical & Procedural Philosophy
A statistical flag from Benford's test is a diagnostic indicator for targeted manual inspection, NEVER an accusation of criminal fraud. The screening tool serves as a precision sieve to optimize scarce audit hours.

7.1 The 5-Stage Audit Screening Architecture

  1. Stage 1: Pre-Screening Suitability & Boundary Assessment: Validate that values represent unconstrained, positive continuous or discrete ratio-scale quantities spanning at least three orders of magnitude. Ensure data does not represent assigned identifiers, exam scores, or fixed retail price tiers.
  2. Stage 2: Standardized Sanitization & Digit Extraction: Process data using scientific notation extraction (`formatC(abs(x), format="e")`). Log and segregate zero/non-finite records.
  3. Stage 3: Dual-Metric Statistical Evaluation: Compute both the sample-size-dependent test statistic (χ², df = 8, p-value) and scale-free effect size (MAD). Check Cochran's rule (E9 ≥ 5).
  4. Stage 4: Automated Risk Triage & Classification: Route ledgers into Green (Compliant: p ≥ 0.05, MAD ≤ 0.015), Yellow (Marginal: review selection filters), or Red (Anomalous: p < 0.01, MAD > 0.015) queues.
  5. Stage 5: Residual-Driven Substantive Forensic Sampling: Rather than inefficient random sampling, auditors inspect transactions originating in digit bins exhibiting the highest positive standardized residuals:
    Ri = (Oi - Ei) / √(Ei(1 - pi))
    Targeting bins with Ri > +2.0 focuses audit resources precisely where fabrication occurs.
